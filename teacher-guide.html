28 Days: Teacher Guide

Classroom Edition. A free game that teaches the menstrual cycle to middle school health classes.

Open the game Home page

28 Days was made by a high school student, with help from an AI coding assistant. Every health fact in it was checked against pages from the CDC, ACOG and NIH, and shows its source (see Sources).

Start here: the 5-minute version

  1. Try it. Play Day 1 yourself at learn28days.org. It takes about 10 to 15 minutes.
  2. Check it at school. Open it on a student device on the school network. If it's blocked, ask IT to allow learn28days.org.
  3. Tell families if your district requires it. A letter template is in Section 10.
  4. In class, students go to learn28days.org, press Play the game, then Start the story (or Choose a day). The game shows them what to do next.
  5. Afterwards, discuss the day's "Think about it" question. At the end of the game, students can print their report card to hand in.

That's all you need to get started. The rest of this guide is there when you want it:

Start here · Section 1

At a glance

Students play Ruby, a middle schooler whose first period starts in the middle of health class. Over one full cycle they pack her bag, sit through health lessons with a pop quiz each school day, handle leaks and cramps, buy supplies, and log each day in a cycle tracker. Three boys in the story learn alongside her.

Audience

Middle school health classes (about grades 6 to 8). Written for everyone in the class, not only students who menstruate.

Time

One class period up to a full unit. The game is split into 8 in-game days you can start on separately.

Cost

Free. No ads.

Devices

Any up-to-date web browser. Works with a keyboard, a mouse or a touch screen. Phones must be turned sideways.

Accounts

None. Nothing to install.

Student data

None collected. Visits are counted anonymously. See Section 13.

Assessment

5 pop quizzes and an 8-question unit test in the game, plus a printable worksheet and exit ticket.

Sources

CDC, ACOG, NIH and others. Every fact in the game shows its source.

Start here · Section 2

Before you teach

  • Play Day 1 yourself. It takes about 10 to 15 minutes and shows you every part of the game. Time it, since your students' pace will be similar.
  • Follow your district's notification policy. Many districts require notice to families, or an opt-out, for puberty education. A family letter template is in Section 10.
  • Test it on a student device, on the school network. Some school filters block new websites. If the game doesn't load, ask your IT staff to allow learn28days.org. See Section 13.
  • Decide on grouping. Students can play alone or in pairs. Pairs work well: one drives, one reads, then they swap each day.
  • Set ground rules before you start (see Section 7).
  • Prepare an anonymous question box. Dr. Flo uses one in the game, so it's an easy idea to borrow.
  • Print what you need: the student quick-start card, the worksheet, or the exit ticket.

How students play

Getting started

  1. Go to learn28days.org and press Play the game.
  2. Choose Start the story for Day 1, or Choose a day to start on a later day. Later days start with Ruby's supplies already stocked.
  3. Each morning a Today's plan card shows what happens and when.

What's on the screen

  • Top left: Ruby's health, energy, mood and cravings, her money, and (during her period) a meter showing how full her pad or tampon is.
  • Top middle: the current goal, with the next timed event under it. A yellow arrow in the world points to where to go.
  • Top right: the cycle clock (all 28 days, colored by phase), the day and time, and the Bag, Phone, Skip and Help buttons.

What happens each day

  1. Home: pack. Ruby's bag holds only six things. What students pack decides how the day goes.
  2. School: learn. Health class with Dr. Flo: a short lesson, then a 3-question quiz with an explanation after each answer.
  3. The rest of the day. Friends, leaks, cramps, the store, swim practice. "Did you know?" cards appear when something related happens. In free time, the Skip button jumps ahead.
  4. Bedtime: log. Students log Ruby's flow, cramps and mood in a cycle tracker. A recap card then lists what the day taught and asks one "Think about it" question.

Key mechanics, briefly

MechanicWhat it teaches
The pad meter and leaksProducts fill up, faster on heavy days. With no product, or a full one, Ruby leaks. Students fix it with spare pants, Dr. Flo or a dispenser. Leaks are treated as normal and fixable.
The six-item bagPlanning ahead, and the trade-offs between products, snacks and spare clothes.
Cramps and cravingsWhat helps, including heat, exercise, snacks, and a pain reliever with a parent's okay.
The cycle trackerLogging a cycle so you can predict the next period. Accurate logs raise a Body Literacy score.
The four hormonesEstrogen, Progesterone, LH and FSH appear as characters at night to explain what's happening.
Grandma OkaforA neighbor in the park who tells six sourced pieces of period history.

Saving and shared computers

Progress saves at the start of each day, in that browser only. On a shared computer, "Continue" picks up whatever game was last saved there; students can choose "Start the story" or "Choose a day" to begin fresh.

Teaching · Section 4

Learning objectives

By the end of the game, students will be able to:

  1. Explain what a period is and why it happens.
  2. Name the four phases of the menstrual cycle in order, and the hormone most linked to each.
  3. Describe the main period products, how they differ, and how often each should be changed.
  4. Identify common causes of cramps and PMS, and healthy ways to manage them.
  5. Explain why tracking a cycle is useful.
  6. Recognize the signs that mean someone should talk to a doctor about their period.
  7. Name trusted adults they could go to with questions, and describe ways to support a friend.

Health education skills it practices

SkillWhere it shows up in the game
Understanding health conceptsDaily health lessons, the glossary and body diagram, the hormone scenes.
Finding valid health informationEvery fact names its source. The Fact Book collects them.
Communicating with othersRuby tells friends, Mom and Dr. Flo what she needs. Friends respond kindly.
Decision-makingPacking a bag that holds only six things. Choosing what to do in each scene.
Practicing healthy behaviorsChanging products on time, managing cramps, tracking the cycle.
Advocating for healthKnowing when "that's just how periods are" isn't true, and telling a trusted adult.

These follow the usual health education skill areas. Check them against your own state and district standards.

Teaching · Section 5

Lesson plans

Times are estimates. Play a day yourself first to judge your class's pace.

Plan A: one class period (45 to 50 minutes)

5 min
Warm-up. Set ground rules. Ask students to write one question they have about periods for the anonymous question box.
25 min
Play Day 1. Alone or in pairs. Students finish when they reach the "Day 1: what you learned" card at bedtime.
10 min
Discuss. Use the Day 1 question, then one or two from Section 7.
5 min
Exit ticket (Section 10). Answer a few box questions now, or save them for next time.

Plan B: three class periods

Period 1
Days 1 and 2. What a period is; products and cramps. Discuss: who are your trusted adults? How can a friend help?
Period 2
Days 5, 9 and 14. The four phases and hormones. Students label the body diagram on the worksheet.
Period 3
Days 21, 26 and the final day. PMS, tracking and when to get help. The final day includes the in-game unit test. Collect the worksheet.

Plan C: a full unit (5 to 6 periods)

Period 1
Introduction and ground rules. Play Day 1. Discussion.
Period 2
Day 2. Products and cramps. Extension: compare products in a table (cost, how long, swimming).
Period 3
Days 5 and 9. The four phases. Worksheet parts A and B.
Period 4
Days 14 and 21. Ovulation, the luteal phase, energy and mood.
Period 5
Day 26. PMS, tracking, warning signs. Answer the question box.
Period 6
The final day and the unit test. Worksheet parts C to E. Wrap-up discussion.

Extension activities

  • Product research: compare pads, tampons, cups and period underwear on cost per month, how long each lasts and what it's good for.
  • Period history: after meeting Grandma Okafor, research how period products changed over the last 100 years.
  • Design a tracker: design a paper cycle tracker that records the things a doctor would want to know.
  • Letter to a friend: write a short, kind note to a friend who just got their first period.

Teaching · Section 7

Leading discussion

Ground rules

  • No teasing, and no laughing at questions. Every question is a real question.
  • Use the correct words for body parts.
  • No personal questions about classmates, and no one has to share their own experience.
  • What's said in the room stays respectful outside it.

Tips

  • Some students will already have periods and some won't. Talk about "people who get periods" and "a friend", not about anyone in the room.
  • Leaks happen in the game on purpose. The characters respond kindly, which gives you a model to point to.
  • The boys in the story ask questions too. Use them to show that everyone benefits from knowing this.
  • If a student describes a sign from the "when to talk to a doctor" list, follow up privately and point them to the school nurse.

Discussion questions

Each day ends with one "Think about it" question (listed in Section 6). More options:

  • What surprised you most in the game?
  • Ruby's friends reacted in different ways. Which reactions helped, and why?
  • If your period started at school, what are three things you could do?
  • Why might someone say "that's just how periods are" about something that isn't normal? What would you say back?
  • What's one thing you'd put in a school period kit, and why?

Common student questions

Short answers you can use. Sources are in the Fact Book and in Section 15.

When do people usually get their first period?
Most girls start between ages 12 and 13, but some start earlier or later. If someone hasn't started by 15, it's worth seeing a doctor. (ACOG)
How long does a period last?
Anywhere from 2 to 7 days is normal. More than 7 days is a reason to talk to a doctor. (ACOG)
Are periods supposed to come every 28 days?
28 days is the average, but for teens anything from 21 to 45 days is normal, and cycles are often irregular for the first few years. (ACOG)
Does it hurt?
Many people get cramps, usually for a day or two. Heat, exercise, sleep and pain relievers help. Pain that keeps someone home from school isn't something to put up with: tell a doctor. (ACOG)
What if my period starts at school?
Go to the nurse or a trusted adult. Many schools have products available. Keeping a pad in your bag helps.
Can you swim on your period?
Yes. Tampons and menstrual cups work in the water; pads don't. (Nemours KidsHealth, ACOG)
How often do you change a pad or tampon?
A pad at least every 4 to 8 hours, or when it feels full. A tampon every 4 to 8 hours, and never longer than 8. (ACOG, CDC)
Do boys get periods? Why do they need to learn this?
No, but everyone has friends and family who do. Knowing the facts makes it easier to be supportive and to recognize when someone needs help.
When should someone see a doctor about their period?
See the list in Section 12, for example needing to change a pad every 1 to 2 hours, or a period longer than 7 days. (CDC, ACOG)

Questions about sex, contraception or pregnancy are outside this game. Answer them according to your district's policy.

Teaching · Section 8

Assessment and answer key

What's built into the game

  • Pop quizzes: 3 questions in each of the 5 health lessons, with an explanation after every answer.
  • Unit test: 8 questions on the final day, with a letter grade.
  • Report card: at the end, students see their quiz total, unit test grade, accurate tracker logs and facts found. Students can press Print my report card to print a one-page summary with their name, scores and tracker log to hand in.

Quick rubric for the exit ticket or discussion

Almost thereMostly accurate, with one gap (for example, can't name a warning sign).
Needs reviewSeveral inaccuracies. Revisit Day 1 and the Unit 5 lesson.

Teaching · Section 9

Differentiation and access

Supports

  • Pairs: one student reads aloud while the other plays.
  • The glossary on Ruby's phone defines 28 words, with a labeled body diagram.
  • Start on any day so absent students can catch up, or so the class can focus on one topic.
  • No time pressure in dialogue: text waits until the student moves on.
  • The Skip button jumps past free time to the next planned event.

Extensions

See the extension activities in Section 5, and challenge students to find all the facts in the Fact Book.

Accessibility

  • Playable with a keyboard, a mouse, or a touch screen.
  • No sound is needed. Sound starts off.
  • Colors are always paired with words or numbers (the stats have labels and values).

Known limits

  • It doesn't work with screen readers.
  • On a phone, it must be turned sideways, and text is small. Tablets, Chromebooks and computers work best.
  • Reading level is roughly middle school; some lesson slides are dense.

Reference · Section 11

Content notes

What it covers

The biology of the menstrual cycle, period products and hygiene, cramps, PMS, mood changes, cycle tracking, and when to see a doctor.

What it doesn't

Sexual activity, contraception, or pregnancy. Pregnancy is mentioned only to explain why the lining of the uterus builds up each cycle.

Language

The game uses the correct names for body parts (uterus, ovaries, cervix, vagina), as health textbooks do. Medicine is always given with a parent's okay or through the health office.

Simplifications

  • The game uses a 28-day cycle with four tidy phases. Real cycles vary, and for teens 21 to 45 days is normal.
  • Doctors count the follicular phase from Day 1, so it overlaps with the period. The game shows them separately, as many textbooks do.
  • The numbers inside the game, such as how fast a pad fills, were chosen for gameplay.
  • Energy and mood changes across the cycle differ a lot between people. The game says "some people".

28 Days is not medical advice. Students with questions about their own bodies should talk to a parent or guardian, the school nurse, or a doctor.

    Privacy and tech details

    Privacy

    • No accounts, logins, names or personal information. Students never type anything about themselves.
    • No cookies and no ads.
    • Saving: progress is stored only in the browser on that device.
    • Visit counting: the site counts visits anonymously with GoatCounter, a privacy-focused counter, so its author can see how often it's used. It stores totals such as which page was opened, browser, screen width and approximate country, plus when a game is started or finished. It doesn't store IP addresses or anything in the browser. If your network blocks it, the game works the same.

    The full privacy page and terms of use can be shared with your district.

    Reference · Section 14

    FAQ

    Is it really free?
    Yes. No ads, no accounts, no paid version.
    How long does it take?
    About 10 to 15 minutes per in-game day, and there are 8 days. Play one yourself to judge your class's pace.
    Can students start in the middle?
    Yes. "Choose a day" on the title screen starts any day with supplies stocked.
    Can I see students' scores?
    Not online, since nothing is collected. At the end, students can print their report card (or save it as a PDF) and hand it in.
    Is it okay for boys to play?
    Yes, and it's designed for that. Three boys in the story learn alongside Ruby.
    Does it cover sex or contraception?
    No. See Section 11.
    A student found a mistake. What should I do?
    Please email the author (Section 15). Corrections from teachers and nurses are very welcome.
    Can I share it with families?
    Yes. The home page, learn28days.org, explains the game for anyone.
    Can I copy or change the handouts?
    Yes. Everything is licensed under CC BY-NC 4.0: share and adapt it freely, with credit to "28 Days by Swara Palanivel", as long as it isn't sold. See the terms of use.
